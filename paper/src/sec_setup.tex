\section{Natural Benchmarks for Image VFL}\label{sec:setup}

A benchmark for image VFL needs entities observed independently by several parties, aligned and labelled. Such data are hard to share, so most image VFL benchmarks cut one image into pieces or render views of one object~\cite{zou2024vflair,wu2024vertibench,wu2015modelnet}. These splits are convenient, but they create the VFL problem rather than sample it: the parties are pieces of one photograph or renderings of one object, complementary by construction, and the best a federation can do is put the object back together. In real VFL each party holds a complete observation, made with its own sensor, device or date: the parties may be redundant, like satellite revisits, or complementary, like two photographs of a lesion, and the question is what each view adds. We therefore use three benchmarks whose parties see the same entity through different sensors, dates or devices (Table~\ref{tab:bench}): one we reuse, reproduce and audit, one we bring to VFL, and one we build.

\paragraph{VertiBench Satellite, reproduced and audited.}\phantomsection\label{sec:vb-audit}
Each of the 16 parties holds one Sentinel-2 revisit of 3{,}927 areas, labelled by the source list the area was sampled from (4 classes; majority class \majVB\,\%). We keep the official split (3{,}142 training and \giVbNTest{} test samples). The parties are largely redundant: one gives \soloMean\,\% accuracy on average, all 16 give \partiesKAll\,\%. We reproduce the published split-learning reference, SplitNN: $\reproAcc\pm\reproStd$\,\%, in line with the published value (App.~\ref{app:vb}). Our audit finds a weakness of the test split. Some sites are cut into $3\times3$ tiles, each one an area of the benchmark; no tile is in both splits, but \auSibShare\,\% of the test tiles have a \emph{sibling}: a training tile cut from the same site. A model that remembers sites can score well on these tiles without learning the classes. With predictions averaged over three seeds (App.~\ref{app:audit}), SplitNN is indeed more accurate on test tiles with a sibling than on the others (\auSnSib{} vs.\ \auSnNoSib\,\%), whereas the one-upload CLIP-L head is not (\auClipSib{} vs.\ \auClipNoSib\,\%). Siblings occur only in the two classes whose sites are tiled, so this cannot be separated from a class effect; we also report results on the tiles without siblings (App.~\ref{app:audit}).

\paragraph{MILK10k: two photographs of the same lesion.}
MILK10k~\cite{milk10k} pairs, for \milkN{} skin lesions, a clinical close-up taken with an ordinary camera (party~A) and a dermoscopic image taken with a dermatoscope (party~B), with patient metadata (optional party~C) and a confirmed diagnosis (7 classes). To our knowledge it has not been used for VFL, yet it is a natural case: the two photographs come from different devices, as a general practitioner and a dermatologist would hold them, each is informative alone, and they complement each other: with late fusion, the two parties together reach \mfGLate\,\% macro F1, against \mfGDerm\,\% for the dermoscopy alone (DINOv2-g). The complementarity is bounded: the two parties are wrong together on \mfGBoth\,\% of the lesions, against \mfGIndep\,\% if their errors were independent (App.~\ref{app:milk}). Without patient identifiers, the five stratified folds are drawn over lesions; with 40 to 61 lesions of the two rarest classes per test fold, macro F1 is the metric.

\paragraph{WorldStrat: two sensors.}
Our two-party task pairs, on \wsIpccN{} WorldStrat~\cite{cornebise2022worldstrat} areas (\wsLccsN{} for land cover), a 1.5\,m SPOT image (party~A) with 16 Sentinel-2 revisits (party~B), labelled by IPCC land-use category (7 classes), land cover~\cite{esa2017cci} (18) or degree of urbanisation~\cite{pesaresi2019ghsl} (8). Stratified folds put neighbouring tiles of a site in training and test and overstate macro F1 by about 6 to 9 points on land cover, so we group folds by site. Fusing the two sensors then reaches \wsIpccFus, \wsLccsFus{} and \wsSmodFus\,\% macro F1 on IPCC, land cover and urbanisation, against \wsIpccSms, \wsLccsSms{} and \wsSmodSpot\,\% for the better sensor alone: the second sensor adds little, so this task tests how to fuse two sensors rather than which VFL protocol to use, and we keep it in App.~\ref{app:ws}. VertiBench Satellite is built from the same WorldStrat areas, so the two satellite benchmarks are not independent replications.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
\emph{Baselines} on raw images: the reproduced VertiBench SplitNN (one ResNet-18 per party trained from random initialisation, 13 bands, 50 epochs); our split-learning recipe with ResNet-18 bottoms from random initialisation or ImageNet weights; FedOnce~\cite{wu2022fedonce} with Noise As Targets~\cite{bojanowski2017nat} or SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simclr}; and split learning with ViT-B foundation bottoms (10 epochs, augmentation). None was tuned extensively. \emph{Encoders}~\cite{oquab2024dinov2,radford2021clip,zhai2023siglip,wang2023ssl4eo,zhang2023biomedclip,kim2024monet} take 224\,px RGB input, 13 bands for the satellite-pretrained SSL4EO-S12 (list in App.~\ref{app:vb}). CLIP ViT-L/14 is the main VertiBench encoder (most accurate alone, with a public decoder); DINOv2 ViT-g/14 serves for compression, missing parties and MILK10k; adapted ViT-B backbones are compared with the same ViT-B frozen. \emph{Statistics:} on VertiBench, mean\,$\pm$\,std over five seeds on the fixed test set (three for ViT split learning, the ImageNet-initialised recipe and the SplitNN re-run); to compare two methods on the same test images, we average each one's predictions over three seeds and apply an exact McNemar test with a paired bootstrap interval, then a Holm correction, which tightens the threshold of each test so that all the comparisons together keep a 5\,\% risk of a false positive. On MILK10k and WorldStrat: five folds, paired by fold.
