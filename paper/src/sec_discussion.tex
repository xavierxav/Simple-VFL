\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
A single upload of frozen foundation features is a strong and cheap baseline for aligned, complete image-VFL splits: it needs no party-side training or training interaction after the upload, its communication does not depend on re-trainings, and each party can compress its upload alone. It is not always the most accurate option: on MILK10k, LoRA gains $\fzGapCb$ to $\fzGapB$ F1 points over the one-shot MLP and $\fzGapViewsCb$ to $\fzGapViewsB$ over precomputed augmentation and late fusion. The paired re-run shows gains from LoRA under the same split-learning recipe, but the larger gain for CLIP-B partly reflects its weaker frozen baseline under that recipe (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}). We do not claim a new learning algorithm, the first use of frozen backbones in federated learning~\cite{hu2023flimages}, or a formal privacy guarantee for the plain upload.

\paragraph{Caveats on the comparisons.}
Our split-learning and FedOnce baselines follow published or standard recipes without extensive hyper-parameter search. On split CIFAR and ModelNet40, the published numbers come from small encoders trained from scratch, and the classes are central to web-scale pre-training data: the margin mostly measures pre-training, not the VFL protocol. Each MILK10k test fold holds only 40 to 61 lesions of the two rarest classes. We report variability across seeds or folds and pair key comparisons by test predictions or by fold. The pixel-loss inversion attack and the plaintext emulation of encrypted training give, respectively, a lower bound on leakage and an upper bound on the accuracy of a deployed protocol.

\paragraph{Data limitations.}
All our federations share a fixed, fully aligned sample set; entity resolution, partial overlap and parties that join late are not studied. VertiBench's 16 parties observe the same sensor at different dates and are largely redundant; its official split does not isolate sites. WorldStrat's site-grouped evaluation avoids that overlap, but labels describe only the centre of each area and are predicted better from the full image than from the smallest crop. MILK10k gives one dermoscopic and one clinical image per lesion; other medical splits (imaging and reports, or different hospitals' modalities) may behave differently.

\paragraph{Privacy.}
Uploads of a public frozen encoder are not private. A public generator decodes some content, the nearest public training image often reveals the label, and matching re-encoded candidate images can identify the source even under some preprocessing changes. These attacks only bound leakage from below. The tested transforms preserve much of the leaked information; per-party clipping and noise degrade accuracy even at large nominal privacy budgets. Encryption is mature only for linear heads: the closed-form protocol reveals second-order feature statistics to the label owner, does not protect the trained model or its predictions, and costs the square of the total feature dimension, which forces a per-party PCA in large federations.

\paragraph{Cost of the frozen backbone.}
Each party must run the encoder once on all its images. A large encoder is not cheaper in FLOPs than training a small bottom model, but this compute is local, needs no synchronisation, is paid once and does not grow with re-trainings; parties without accelerators can use encoders of 30 to 50 GFLOPs per image at a cost of one to two points. The encoders we use are trained on RGB photographs; domain-specific encoders (multispectral satellite or dermatology models) slot into the same protocol and are the natural way to reduce the price of freezing.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All code, configurations, raw result files and the scripts that generate every table and figure from them are in the accompanying repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/xavierxav/Simple-VFL}, directories \texttt{paper\_experiments} and \texttt{paper\_results}.}. All data sets are public. Each frozen head trains in seconds to minutes on one GPU; the whole study, including the end-to-end baselines, the image augmentation and the WorldStrat feature extraction, used about 110 Google Colab compute units on L4 and A100 GPUs.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
For aligned image VFL, a single upload of frozen foundation-model features is a strong, cheap and simple baseline. On VertiBench Satellite, the frozen CLIP heads outperform the reproduced SplitNN in the paired audit, and all frozen foundation-model heads in Table~\ref{tab:main} outperform our self-supervised one-shot baselines. The frozen CLIP-B head is close in accuracy to the limited-seed LoRA result, and the one-shot method exceeds published results on split CIFAR and ModelNet40. It loses accuracy against end-to-end adaptation on dermatology images. Its upload compresses to a few hundred bytes without coordination, tolerates missing parties with a pooling head, and supports encrypted training of a linear head. With folds grouped by site, multispectral Sentinel-2 revisits help land-cover classification, but their concatenation with SPOT does not improve urbanisation; on MILK10k, late fusion better exploits the two photographs of a lesion. We suggest that image VFL methods report this baseline, and the price of freezing, alongside their own results.
