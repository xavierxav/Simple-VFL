\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
A single upload of frozen foundation features is a strong and cheap baseline for aligned, complete image-VFL splits: it needs no party-side training and no interaction, its communication does not depend on the number of epochs or re-trainings, and each party can compress its upload alone. It is not always the most accurate option: when the parties' images differ from web photographs and a few thousand labels are available, adapting the encoder end-to-end with LoRA gains \fzGapCb{} to \fzGapB{} points over the same frozen head, and \fzGapViewsCb{} to \fzGapViewsB{} over frozen features with precomputed augmentation and late fusion (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}), and a VFL method should be expected to beat the frozen baseline by about that margin, at its own cost in communication and synchronisation. We do not claim a new learning algorithm, the first use of frozen backbones in federated learning~\cite{hu2023flimages}, or a formal privacy guarantee for the plain upload.

\paragraph{Caveats on the comparisons.}
Our split-learning and FedOnce baselines follow the published or standard recipes without extensive hyper-parameter search; a better-tuned end-to-end model could do better, which is why we also reproduce the published VertiBench reference. On split CIFAR and ModelNet40, the published numbers come from small encoders trained from scratch, and the classes are central to web-scale pre-training data: the margin there mostly measures pre-training, not the VFL protocol. The VertiBench test set has 785 samples and each MILK10k test fold holds only 40 to 61 lesions of the two rarest classes, so differences of one point are within test-sampling noise; we therefore report the standard deviation over seeds or folds and pair the key comparisons by test predictions or by fold. The pixel-loss inversion attack and the plaintext emulation of encrypted training give, respectively, a lower bound on leakage and an upper bound on the accuracy of a deployed protocol.

\paragraph{Data limitations.}
All our federations share a fixed, fully aligned sample set; entity resolution, partial overlap and parties that join late are not studied. VertiBench's 16 parties observe the same sensor at different dates and are largely redundant. WorldStrat's labels are read at the centre of each area, from a 300\,m land-cover pixel or a 1\,km settlement cell, so they describe a small part of the imaged area, are predicted better from its surroundings than from a close-up, and limit what any sensor can add. MILK10k gives one dermoscopic and one clinical image per lesion; other medical splits (imaging and reports, or different hospitals' modalities) may behave differently.

\paragraph{Privacy.}
Uploads of a public frozen encoder are not private. A public generator already decodes the content of CLIP uploads, fine-tuning it on public in-domain images makes its outputs realistic but no closer to the source, the nearest public image in feature space reveals the label as well as the image itself, and matching the uploads against candidate images links them exactly. Our attacks only bound the leakage from below: a better decoder, or one trained for the pooled DINOv2 token, can only extract more. Transforms that keep the accuracy keep this information, so protection has to come from noise, which destroys accuracy at useful privacy levels for a single upload, or from encryption, which is mature only for linear heads, reveals the second-order statistics of the features to the label owner in the closed-form protocol, does not protect the trained model or its predictions, and costs the square of the total feature dimension, which forces a per-party PCA in large federations.

\paragraph{Cost of the frozen backbone.}
Each party must run the encoder once on all its images. A large encoder is not cheaper in FLOPs than training a small bottom model, but this compute is local, needs no synchronisation, is paid once and does not grow with re-trainings; parties without accelerators can use encoders of 30 to 50 GFLOPs per image at a cost of one to two points. The encoders we use are trained on RGB photographs; domain-specific encoders (multispectral satellite or dermatology models) slot into the same protocol and are the natural way to reduce the price of freezing.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All code, configurations, raw result files and the scripts that generate every table and figure from them are in the accompanying repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/xavierxav/Simple-VFL}, directories \texttt{paper\_experiments} and \texttt{paper\_results}.}. All data sets are public. Each frozen head trains in seconds to minutes on one GPU; the whole study, including the end-to-end baselines, the image augmentation and the WorldStrat feature extraction, used about 110 Google Colab compute units on L4 and A100 GPUs.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
For aligned image VFL, a single upload of frozen foundation-model features is a strong, cheap and simple baseline. It outperforms split learning and self-supervised one-shot VFL on VertiBench Satellite, exceeds published results on split CIFAR and ModelNet40, and loses 1 to 7 points against end-to-end adaptation of the same backbone on dermatology images, depending on the encoder and on whether the frozen parties precompute augmentation. Its upload compresses to a few hundred bytes without coordination, tolerates missing parties with a pooling head, and supports encrypted training of a linear head, at a cost that grows quickly with the feature dimension. Whether a second party helps depends less on the fusion head than on what it adds: Sentinel-2 revisits add infrared and temporal information to a SPOT image for fine land-cover classes but not for urbanisation, and images of the same lesion from two devices complement each other, which late fusion exploits better than a joint head trained on few labels. We suggest that image VFL methods report this baseline, and the price of freezing, alongside their own results.
