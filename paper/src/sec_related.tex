\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Vertical federated learning and communication.}
In vertical federated learning (VFL) several parties hold different features of the same samples and only one of them holds the labels~\cite{liu2024vfl}. The reference training scheme is split learning~\cite{vepakomma2018splitnn}: each party runs a local \emph{bottom} model, sends its activations to the label owner at every mini-batch, and receives gradients back, so that the communication cost grows with the number of epochs, samples and parties. Several lines of work reduce this cost with local updates, compression or asynchrony~\cite{liu2024vfl}. The most radical option is \emph{one-shot} VFL: FedOnce~\cite{wu2022fedonce} lets every passive party learn a representation of its own data with self-supervised learning (SSL), uploads the representations once, and trains the top model at the label owner. The price is that every party must run an SSL pre-training on (often small) local data. Open libraries such as VFLAIR~\cite{zou2024vflair} and benchmarks such as VertiBench~\cite{wu2024vertibench} make these methods comparable; FeT~\cite{wu2024fet} and LASER-VFL~\cite{valdeira2025laser} relax the alignment and full-participation assumptions that we keep, and \cite{wu2025vflpractice} documents how far typical benchmarks are from deployable settings.

\paragraph{Pre-trained backbones in federated learning.}
Frozen pre-trained backbones are hard to beat in \emph{horizontal} federated learning on images~\cite{hu2023flimages}, where they remove most of the training and communication cost. In remote sensing, large-scale pre-training on Sentinel-2~\cite{wang2023ssl4eo} and general-purpose vision foundation models~\cite{oquab2024dinov2,simeoni2025dinov3} provide strong frozen features. To our knowledge, however, the combination ``frozen foundation features as the one-shot upload of a \emph{vertical} split'' has not been systematically studied on an open satellite VFL benchmark, and nothing is known about its accuracy/communication trade-off under compression, about its behaviour when parties are missing, or about how much the uploaded features reveal about the images.

\paragraph{Privacy of uploaded representations.}
Uploading intermediate representations is not private by itself: model-inversion attacks reconstruct inputs from intermediate activations of collaborative inference~\cite{he2019modelinversion} and from image representations in general~\cite{dosovitskiy2016inverting}, and feature-inference attacks target VFL~\cite{luo2021feature}. Formal guarantees such as Gaussian differential privacy~\cite{dong2022gdp,dwork2014algorithmic} require noise levels that are usually costly in utility. We therefore treat privacy \emph{empirically}: we measure how well a decoder trained by the coordinator inverts the uploaded features, as a function of how they are compressed or perturbed, and we state what such a measurement does and does not show.

\paragraph{Evaluation under spatial autocorrelation.}
Random train/test splits of geo-referenced samples overestimate performance when nearby samples share labels, which is why spatial block cross-validation is recommended~\cite{roberts2017crossvalidation,ploton2020spatial}. This matters for the WorldStrat-based benchmark~\cite{cornebise2022worldstrat} used here, whose labels are partly determined by where the images were taken.
